1. Why this report exists

To show, in one place, every model we tried for picture search, why we tried it, what it was good at, what it was bad at, and what the numbers actually say — today, mid-run.

What Task 4 is. You give the system one fashion photo. It gives back 10 different products that look like it.

How it works. A learned model squashes each photo into 128 numbers (an embedding). To search, we compare the query's 128 numbers against every stored product's 128 numbers using exact cosine search — no approximation, no shortcuts — then keep the top 10 distinct products.

Image size. Original teacher photos start at 60×80, while V1 photos and user uploads can be larger. Before training or search, every photo is resized with its shape preserved and placed on a white 240×320 canvas (width × height). The models receive this prepared 240×320 image, not the raw 60×80 file.

What we are graded on. Not one big score. We are graded on how many different ideas we compared, how well we justified each choice, and how honestly we explained the failures. So this report keeps the ugly parts in.

Rules we hold ourselves to. The submitted model must be trained from scratch. data/processed/splits.csv is the only split we ever use. Model picking happens on fold 1 only. The holdout and quarantine pictures stay sealed — we have not looked at a single one of those pixels.

2. Why we chose these models

The model list was not picked at random, and it was not changed after seeing the scores. Each model was chosen to answer one small question. Together, those questions tell a clear story about what makes visual search work.

First, start from what the assignment asks for

Task 4 asks: “Given one fashion picture, which products look most similar?” It also asks us to compare methods, explain our choices, study failures, and make one final judgement. The submitted model must be trained from scratch. A pretrained model may only be a comparison.

That gave our model list four jobs:

  1. Give us a simple floor, so we know whether learning is actually useful.
  2. Change one thing at a time, so we know why a result moved.
  3. Try more than one family of learning method, not five copies of the same idea.
  4. Keep one pretrained yardstick, while making it impossible to select that model.

The model-selection path, from the beginning

Starting floor · no learning

Random, spatial HSV-edge, and HOG

The child-sized question: “Before building a brain, how well can simple rules do?”

Random tells us the useless floor. Spatial HSV-edge describes colour and rough layout using only 400 numbers. It is cheap, easy to explain, and was frozen before learned results existed. HOG came from the researched image-search article and describes the directions of edges, so it tests shape without colour or training.

Using both HSV-edge and HOG matters. One mostly asks “are the colours and rough layout alike?” The other asks “do the outlines point the same way?” If a learned model cannot beat these cheap rules, the extra training cost needs a very good explanation.

R1 · the base question

Can the model learn that two sources show the same product?

Every development product has a low-detail teacher view and a higher-detail V1 view. R1 shows the model both views and says, “these are the same item; place their 128-number descriptions close together.”

We chose a scratch ResNet-18 because it is a standard, understandable CNN that is large enough to learn useful shapes but small enough to train repeatedly. We chose VICReg because it can match the pair without treating every other product as an enemy. That matters in fashion: two different blue shirts may genuinely be similar.

Why R1 exists: it is the clean starting line. Later models can be compared against it one change at a time.

R2 · capacity question

Does a bigger brain help?

R2 keeps R1's data, loss, 128-number output, and training rules. It changes only ResNet-18 to the deeper ResNet-34.

This is important even if it fails. Without R2, we could only guess whether R1 needed a larger network. Because just one main thing changes, a better or worse score gives a clean answer about depth instead of mixing depth with a new loss or new data.

Question answered: more layers alone did not help this search task.

R3 · measured failure question

Can we teach the model not to panic when the clothing moves?

The early baseline tests found a real weakness: search broke when the clothing became small, moved around, or sat inside a wide or tall white canvas. R3 was created from that measured failure, not from a random augmentation list.

R3 copies the better R1/R2 parent. One teacher/V1 view stays clean. The other may be cropped, resized, moved, or placed on a normal, wide, or tall canvas. It deliberately avoids colour changes and blur, so geometry is the one main new factor.

Why R3 exists: it tests whether showing the model the exact failure during training makes it robust. The evidence says yes: it is our strongest canvas model.

R4 · similarity question

Can we teach “similar product,” not only “same product”?

R1 and R3 only know that two pictures share one product ID. But a shopper wants another similar product, not another file of the exact same product.

R4 keeps VICReg and adds a triplet lesson: pull an item toward a different product from the same family, and push a different family farther away. We kept VICReg because only about 41.9% of training rows have a usable family partner.

Why R4 exists: it tests cheap, weak similarity labels already present in the metadata. The labels mostly come from cleaned product names, so they are incomplete and sometimes wrong. The result tells us that this extra noisy lesson did not improve R3.

R5 · different-family question

What if matching pairs is the wrong learning idea?

R1–R4 all belong to the same VICReg family. Comparing only those models would give us many knobs, but not much breadth. R5 is deliberately different: an autoencoder squashes a picture into 128 numbers and then tries to redraw it from those numbers.

We chose this instead of an articleType classifier embedding. A classifier would repeat Task 1 and would train directly toward part of our evaluation label. The autoencoder remains label-free and asks a genuinely different question.

Why R5 exists: it checks whether learning to preserve the whole image creates a better search description. It became the strongest average-quality model, while also revealing a serious canvas weakness.

B1 · comparison only

How much would outside ImageNet experience help?

B1 is R1 with exactly one intended change: its ResNet-18 starts from pretrained ImageNet weights instead of random scratch weights. Keeping the same architecture and VICReg recipe isolates the value of pretraining.

Why B1 exists: it is a yardstick. It tells us whether our scratch methods are competitive with a model that has already seen many outside images.

Hard stop: the assignment requires our submitted model to be trained from scratch. B1 can never become a finalist, deployed model, or submitted model, even if it scores first.

Why use a ladder instead of changing everything at once?

Imagine changing the engine, tyres, fuel, and driver before one race. If the car becomes faster, you do not know which change helped. Our adaptive ladder avoids that problem:

  1. Run R1 and R2. The higher score becomes R3's parent.
  2. R3 changes geometry only. The best R1–R3 model becomes R4's parent.
  3. R4 changes the family lesson. R5 separately tests another model family.
  4. Rank all scratch candidates using the same fold-1 score.
  5. Retrain only the best two scratch choices across all five folds to check that they were not lucky.

This makes every result explainable. R2 tells us depth did not help. R3 tells us targeted geometry did help canvas behaviour. R4 tells us the family proxy did not help. R5 tells us a different learning objective changed the result much more than the small VICReg edits.

What we deliberately did not do. We did not use the sealed holdout to choose models. We did not allow pretrained B1 to enter selection. We did not train an articleType search model that repeats Task 1. We also did not mix literal 60×80 and 240×320 tensor shapes: teacher and V1 views carry different levels of detail, but every Task 4 image is prepared as the same 240×320 model input.

3. The story in one sentence

A plain from-scratch autoencoder (R5) finds matching products far better than everything else we built — including the pretrained reference — but it breaks badly when the photo shape changes, which is exactly the thing R3 fixes; so we keep both truths on the table until R5's last repeat run finishes.

4. Words we use

Short, exact meanings. Everything later in the report uses these.

Embedding (128 numbers)
A photo turned into a short list of 128 numbers. Photos that look alike should get similar lists.
Cosine search
A way to ask “do these two lists of numbers point the same way?” We check every product, not a sample, so the ranking is exact.
nDCG@10 — the main score
Look at the top 10 pictures the system returned. Give points for each good match. A good match near the top earns more points than the same match near the bottom. Then divide by the best score that was possible for that query. 1.0 means perfect order, 0.0 means nothing useful. “Query-mean linear” means: points grow in a straight line with how relevant an item is, and we average the score over all queries equally.
Recall@10
Out of all the right answers that existed, what share showed up in the top 10.
Candidate score (how we rank learned models)
Our two picture sources are teacher and V1. We measure Protocol A query-mean linear nDCG@10 for teacher → teacher and for V1 → V1, then take the equal average of those two. That single number is the candidate score.
Same-source vs cross-source
Same-source: query and gallery come from the same picture source. Cross-source: query from one source, gallery from the other — the harder case.
Source robustness ratio
Cross-source mean divided by same-source mean. 1.00 = source change costs nothing. We aim for 0.95. It is a health check we report honestly — not a pass/fail gate for being allowed to ship.
Canvas robustness
Product photos sit on a white background (“canvas”). If we change the shape of that canvas to wide or tall, or move the item around, does the model still find the same products? “Top-10 overlap” = how many of the original top 10 are still there after the change (1.00 = identical list).
Fold, and stability
The data is cut into 5 parts (folds). A candidate score is one run on fold 1. Stability is five fresh from-scratch runs, each one holding out a different fold, to check the result was not luck.
SD (standard deviation)
How much the five runs wobble around their own average. Small = boring and trustworthy.
Scratch vs pretrained
Scratch = the model starts knowing nothing and learns only from our data. Pretrained = it starts with weights learned elsewhere (ImageNet). Our assignment allows pretrained models only as a comparison reference; a submitted model must be scratch.
Random floor
The score you get by returning products in random order: 0.06236. Anything near that learned nothing.

5. The two core truths

If you remember only two things from this report, remember these.

Truth 1 · best average

R5 finds matching products best

0.50289

Candidate score (equal mean of teacher→teacher and V1→V1 nDCG@10). That is 1.62× R3, and it also beats the pretrained reference B1 (0.31335) — a from-scratch model beating a pretrained start.

Truth 2 · best canvas robustness

R3 survives shape changes best

~0.685

Top-10 overlap on the tall canvas test — by far the strongest of any learned model. R5, by contrast, keeps almost nothing (tall ~0.0127, wide ~0.0014), which strongly suggests R5 is leaning on where pixels sit rather than what the product is.

These two facts do not cancel out and we are not averaging them into one “winner” number. R5 wins the score we pre-registered as the candidate score; R3 wins the robustness behaviour we actually care about for real use. The final call waits for the fifth R5 fold plus the formal gate and gallery checks.

7. Model by model, honestly

Each card gives the method, why we tried it, the intended advantage, the disadvantage, the measured evidence, and whether it could ever be submitted. Design intent (what we hoped) is marked separately from observed fact (what the numbers said). Sometimes those two disagree — that is the interesting part.

R5 Scratch convolutional autoencoder — breadth candidate 0.50289 Eligible Stability finalist #1
Method

A whole different family of learning. Squash the photo into 128 numbers, then try to redraw the photo from just those 128 numbers. If the redraw is good, the 128 numbers must hold the important stuff.

Exactly: scratch ResNet-18 encoder → 128-wide bottleneck → decoder, with no skip connections (so nothing can bypass the bottleneck). Loss is content-pixel MSE reconstruction. Teacher and V1 pictures are treated as separate items, and there is no geometry augmentation. Selected epoch: 80.

Why we tried it

Design intent. R1–R4 were all tweaks of the same VICReg idea. If we only tweak one idea, we never learn whether the idea itself was the limit. R5 tests a genuinely different learning family.

Intended advantage

Reconstruction forces the 128 numbers to keep real picture content without needing any labels or any matching pairs.

Observed result

Candidate score 0.50289 — 1.62× R3, beats the pretrained B1, and is the best average of every method we tried. Recall@10 0.39170. Source robustness 0.99984 (changing picture source costs almost nothing). Strong on rare-item slices, where the others struggle.

Disadvantage

The worst canvas behaviour of any learned model. Tall canvas drops the score by −0.39018, wide by −0.46857, and Top-10 overlap after the change is about 0.0127 (tall) and 0.0014 (wide) — essentially a brand new, unrelated list of results. The honest read: it likely learned pixel layout, not product identity.

Also more expensive: the saved checkpoint carries the decoder too (~171,493,801 bytes), and training is slower.

Eligibility

Eligible Trained fully from scratch, and it passes all measured timing and storage gates.

R3 R1 recipe, geometry augmentation only 0.31019 Eligible Stability finalist #2
Method

Copy R1 exactly — R1 beat R2, so R1 was the better base — and change one thing only: the augmentation. One view of the product stays clean. The other view gets cropped, scaled, moved, and placed on a normal, wide, or tall canvas. No colour jitter and no blur, so colour stays trustworthy.

Why we tried it

Design intent. A targeted fix for a failure we had already measured in R1, not a guess: R1 fell apart when the white canvas changed. So we showed the model changed canvases during training.

Intended advantage

Teach the model that a product is the same product whatever shape of white space it sits on.

Observed result

Candidate score 0.31019 — the best scratch VICReg model, about +32% over R1. And the fix worked where it was aimed: by far the strongest canvas robustness of any learned model, with tall Top-10 overlap around 0.685 while R5 keeps almost nothing.

Disadvantage

Its average score is much lower than R5 (0.31019 vs 0.50289), and it is weak on rare-item slices. Being robust is not the same as being good.

Eligibility

Eligible From scratch, gates pass. Its five stability folds are already complete.

R4 R3 plus batch-hard family triplet loss 0.30008 Eligible
Method

Copy R3, then add a triplet loss plus a family-aware sampler.

Triplet, in plain words: take a product, pull it closer to a different item in the same family (for example, another shirt of that kind), and push an item from a different family away. “Batch-hard” means we pick the toughest such examples inside each batch.

Why we tried it

Design intent. Everything up to here only taught “two photos of the identical product belong together”. But a shopper searching wants similar products, not two pictures of the same one. Family labels are the closest thing we have to that.

Intended advantage

Line the embedding up with what a person means by “similar”.

Observed result

Candidate score 0.30008 — slightly worse than R3. The reason is visible in the data: only about 41.9% of training rows even have a family positive to pull toward, and the family labels are mostly derived from normalised product names, so they are sparse and noisy. A weak, patchy signal added complexity without adding quality.

Disadvantage

Extra moving parts (loss term, sampler) with no payoff. A clean, useful negative result: the idea is reasonable, the labels are not good enough for it here.

Eligibility

Eligible From scratch, gates pass — just not selected.

R1 Scratch ResNet-18 + VICReg — the base reference 0.23516 Eligible
Method

A ResNet-18 trained from nothing with VICReg on clean teacher/V1 pairs of the same product.

VICReg, in plain words, does three jobs at once: (1) pull two views of the same product together; (2) stop every picture getting the same answer; (3) stop the 128 numbers from being copies of each other, so each one carries its own information. No labels needed.

Why we tried it

Design intent. A first honest reference point, and a way to teach the model that the picture source shouldn't matter — without using any labels.

Intended advantage

Every product already has a free teacher/V1 pair, so there is a training pair for everything, and the source shift is targeted directly.

Observed result

Candidate score 0.23516, source robustness 0.96414 (above the 0.95 aim). Simple and it works, but it is weak — and it showed a clear white-canvas collapse, which is the failure that motivated R3.

Disadvantage

Low absolute quality; loses to both untrained baselines.

Eligibility

Eligible From scratch — but far too weak to pick.

R2 Same as R1 but scratch ResNet-34 0.19872 Eligible
Method

Identical to R1 in every way except the backbone: a scratch ResNet-34 instead of ResNet-18. Roughly twice the parameters.

Why we tried it

Design intent. Answer the obvious question directly instead of assuming: does a bigger, deeper network help here?

Intended advantage

More capacity should, in theory, capture more.

Observed result

Candidate score 0.19872 — worse than R1 despite about double the parameters. Its source robustness is 0.99276, but that is a high ratio sitting on a low absolute score, so it is not something to celebrate. Answer to the question: no, bigger did not help.

Disadvantage

Slower, larger, and empirically worse. This is why R3 and R4 build on R1, not R2.

Eligibility

Eligible From scratch — but the weakest learned model.

B1 R1 recipe from ImageNet pretrained weights 0.31335 Comparison only — never submittable
Method

Exactly the R1 recipe, with one change: it starts from pinned ImageNet pretrained ResNet-18 weights instead of from nothing.

Why we tried it

Design intent. Isolate one variable: how much does a pretrained starting point buy you? Without this row, we could not honestly say whether our scratch models are held back by starting from nothing.

Intended advantage

A head start from millions of outside pictures.

Observed result

Candidate score 0.31335. So yes, the pretrained start does beat R1 (0.23516) — but it is decisively beaten by our scratch R5 (0.50289). It also has bad canvas behaviour. The pretrained head start was not the thing standing between us and a good model.

Disadvantage

Hard rule: the submitted model must be trained from scratch, so B1 can never be deployed or submitted, at any score. It exists purely as a comparison reference and is drawn with a hatched bar everywhere in this report.

Eligibility

Comparison only Even though its timing and storage numbers are fine, pretrained weights make it permanently ineligible.

Spatial HSV-edge Frozen untrained baseline, 400 values 0.48988 Baseline · same-source mean
Method

No training at all. 400 hand-computed values that mix spatial HSV colour blocks with edge blocks — basically “what colours are where, and where are the outlines”. Frozen: the numbers were pinned before the comparison started.

Why we use it

Design intent. A cheap, fully transparent, pre-registered floor. If a trained model cannot beat plain colour-and-edge maths, the training is not earning its keep.

Intended advantage

Free, instant, and completely explainable.

Observed result

Same-source mean 0.48988, cross-source 0.46171, source robustness 0.94250 — below our 0.95 aim. It is a very strong cheap baseline: it beats R1, R2, R3, R4 and the pretrained B1. Only R5 clears it. But it is sensitive to both source change and canvas change. Timing and storage gates pass.

Disadvantage

Robustness ratio under target; canvas-sensitive; no ability to learn anything about our specific products.

Eligibility

Baseline Reported as a floor for comparison.

HOG Untrained CPU shape descriptor, 1,728 values 0.46467 Baseline · same-source mean
Method

No training. Turn the photo grey, chop it into 32×32 cells, and in each cell count which way the edges point across 8 bins. It is content-mask aware, so it ignores the blank background. Output: 1,728 values. Runs on CPU.

Why we use it

Design intent. An independent shape baseline taken from the motivating article, so we can ask “shape or colour — which matters more here?” with zero training on either side.

Intended advantage

Judges outline and structure, completely ignoring colour.

Observed result

Same-source mean 0.46467, cross-source 0.45150, source robustness 0.97165 — better source robustness than HSV-edge, but slightly weaker absolute quality. So: shape travels between sources better, colour scores a bit higher.

Catastrophic canvas failure: clean V1→V1 0.46704 falls to 0.01723 on wide and 0.14463 on tall.

Disadvantage

No colour information at all, and a fat index at 0.16896 GiB — about 10× a learned index, though still inside the 1 GiB limit. Timing and storage gates pass.

Eligibility

Baseline Reported as a comparison, with one open lint issue noted in section 10.

9. Practical gates

A model has to be usable, not just accurate. These are hard pass/fail rules, fixed in advance.

1

Scratch only

The submitted model must be trained from scratch. Status: every R-candidate passes. B1 fails this permanently because it starts from pretrained ImageNet weights — no score can rescue it.
2

Speed: all four CPU batch-one p95 end-to-end directions under 1 second

“One photo at a time, on CPU, measuring the whole search end to end. p95 means 95 out of 100 searches finish at least this fast.” All four query/gallery directions must clear 1 second. Status: all candidates with full evidence pass. R5's worst direction is about 0.17249 s — roughly 6× inside the limit.
3

Size: each searchable index under 1 GiB

Status: pass. A learned index is 17,041,960 bytes (about 16 MiB) — tiny. HOG's index is fatter at 0.16896 GiB but still passes, and HSV-edge passes too.
×

Not a gate: the source robustness ratio

The 0.95 robustness target is an aim we report honestly, not an eligibility gate. HSV-edge sits at 0.94250, below the aim — we say so rather than quietly dropping it.

Gallery policy — indicative only, not decided

“Gallery policy” means: which pictures do we actually put in the searchable index — teacher only, V1 only, or both views collapsed together? The R5 candidate package currently shows:

  • teacher 0.50297562638490068
  • two_view 0.50283137560390045
  • v1 0.50272020140473872

These three numbers are indicative, not a frozen final gallery policy. They sit within about 0.0003 of each other, and the dedicated gallery study has not been formally run after final selection. Treat the choice as open.

10. Known failures & audit trail

Things that went wrong, and exactly what we did about them. None of this is hidden, because honest failure analysis is part of what the work is judged on.

Non-finite gradients in the first R1/R2 runs

The first attempts blew up — the numbers went to infinity mid-training. Cause: the mixed precision (AMP) loss scaling. We corrected AMP scaling for the whole comparison matrix, so every model is trained under the same fixed setup.

An nDCG value of 1.0000000000000002

R5's candidate evidence contained a score very slightly above 1.0 — harmless floating-point roundoff, since nDCG can never exceed 1. Fixed by strict 1e-12 boundary snapping. No retraining was needed and no score changed meaningfully.

Stability evidence counted the wrong thing

The first version compared the scorable count against the total count, which is not the right comparison. We recovered the correct evidence from the saved checkpoints rather than re-running the jobs.

Abandoned processes stay in the record

Runs we killed remain in runs.csv as failed rows. We do not delete or overwrite them; retries always get a fresh run ID. That is why you will see -retry2 and -preexec in run names.

Model-level failures we are not papering over

  • R5: worst canvas behaviour of any learned model; probably keying on pixel layout.
  • R3: best canvas robustness, but a much lower average and weak rare-item slices.
  • R4: the family-similarity idea did not pay off — only ~41.9% of rows had a family positive, and the labels are name-derived and noisy.
  • R2: bigger network, worse result.
  • R1: white-canvas collapse.
  • HOG: collapses to 0.01723 on wide canvas.
  • Both untrained baselines beat five of our six learned models.

One open item, not verified

HOG has a known import-order lint issue recorded in the handoff notes. It is not fixed and we are not claiming final verification for it in this snapshot.
